JOSE RIZAL: NATIONAL HERO OF THE PHILIPPINES
=============================================

EARLY LIFE

Jose Protasio Rizal Mercado y Alonso Realonda was born on June 19, 1861, in Calamba, Laguna, as the seventh of eleven children of Francisco Rizal Mercado and Teodora Alonzo. His family were prosperous landowners. From an early age, he showed exceptional intellectual ability — he reportedly learned the alphabet at age three and could read at age five.

He completed his early education in Binan, Laguna, and then studied at the Ateneo Municipal de Manila, graduating with highest honors. He then enrolled at the University of Santo Tomas to study medicine, but left for Spain in 1882 to continue his studies at the Universidad Central de Madrid, where he earned degrees in medicine and philosophy.

He was fluent in over twenty languages, including Spanish, French, German, English, Latin, Greek, Arabic, Malay, Tagalog, and several other Philippine languages.

THE PROPAGANDA MOVEMENT

During his years in Europe (1882-1892), Rizal became the most prominent voice in the Propaganda Movement — a campaign by reform-minded Filipinos living in Spain who used writing and journalism to expose the abuses of Spanish colonial rule and the Catholic friar orders. He contributed extensively to La Solidaridad, the movement's newspaper published in Barcelona.

He published an annotated edition of Antonio de Morga's Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas in 1890, adding his own commentary to document Philippine civilization before Spanish colonization. His goal was to show that Filipinos had a rich, complex culture that predated Spanish arrival — a direct counter to the colonial argument that Spain had "civilized" a backward people.

NOLI ME TANGERE (1887)

While in Berlin, Rizal published Noli Me Tangere (Latin for "Touch Me Not") in 1887. The title comes from the Gospel of John — the words of the risen Christ to Mary Magdalene — which Rizal used to symbolize his country's longing for liberation.

The novel is set in the Philippines during Spanish colonial rule. Its protagonist, Juan Crisostomo Ibarra, returns from Europe with ideas for educational reform, only to find his plans blocked by corrupt Spanish friars and colonial officials. The story exposes the abuses of the friar orders, the discrimination against native Filipinos, and the gap between colonial rhetoric and colonial reality.

The novel caused an immediate uproar. The Spanish authorities labeled it seditious. The Catholic Church condemned it. Copies were banned and those caught with the book faced arrest. Despite this — or because of it — the novel spread widely through underground networks.

EL FILIBUSTERISMO (1891)

The sequel, El Filibusterismo (The Filibustering, also known as The Reign of Greed), is darker in tone. The protagonist Simoun, a jeweler, returns to the Philippines planning violent revolution rather than reform. The novel is widely read as Rizal's warning: if peaceful reform is denied, violence becomes inevitable.

Together, Noli and El Fili are required reading in Philippine schools under Republic Act 1425 (the Rizal Law, 1956).

LA LIGA FILIPINA AND EXILE

In July 1892, Rizal returned to the Philippines and founded La Liga Filipina, a civic organization advocating peaceful reform. Three days later, he was arrested on charges of sedition and exiled to Dapitan, a remote town in Mindanao, where he remained for four years.

During his exile, Rizal practiced medicine, established a school, built a water system, and continued his scientific research. He discovered several new species of animals and named them after Filipino naturalists.

ARREST, TRIAL, AND EXECUTION

When the Philippine Revolution broke out in August 1896, the Spanish colonial government blamed Rizal despite his having had no direct involvement. He was arrested, transported back to Manila, and tried before a military tribunal on charges of rebellion, sedition, and conspiracy.

Rizal consistently denied advocating armed revolt. He condemned the revolution in writing, arguing it was premature. Nevertheless, on December 30, 1896, he was executed by firing squad at Bagumbayan (now Rizal Park) in Manila. He was 35 years old.

His execution backfired on the Spanish. Rather than silencing the independence movement, it turned Rizal into a martyr and galvanized Filipino resistance. Within two years, Spain's grip on the Philippines had collapsed.

LEGACY

Rizal was not a revolutionary in the armed sense — he advocated reform through education, civic engagement, and peaceful political pressure. Historians have debated whether he would have approved of the revolution that bore indirect fruit from his writings.

What is not debated is his impact. His novels gave Filipinos a mirror — a way to see the injustices of their situation clearly described in literature. His life gave them a model: a Filipino who was, by any measure, the intellectual equal of anyone in the world.

He is considered the national hero of the Philippines. December 30, the date of his execution, is observed as Rizal Day, a national holiday. His face appears on the one-peso coin and the most prominent park in Manila bears his name.
