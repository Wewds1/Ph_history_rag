SPANISH COLONIZATION OF THE PHILIPPINES (1565-1898)
=====================================================

MAGELLAN'S ARRIVAL (1521)

Ferdinand Magellan, a Portuguese explorer sailing under the Spanish crown, arrived in the Philippine archipelago in March 1521. He landed first in Homonhon island in the Eastern Visayas, then sailed to Limasawa where he forged an alliance with local ruler Rajah Kolambu. Moving on to Cebu, Magellan converted Rajah Humabon and hundreds of his subjects to Christianity.

Magellan's alliance with Humabon drew him into a local conflict. Datu Lapulapu of Mactan island refused to submit to Spanish authority or to Humabon. On April 27, 1521, Magellan led a force of around 60 men against Lapulapu's warriors on the beach of Mactan. He was killed in the fighting. His surviving crew eventually completed the first circumnavigation of the globe, returning to Spain in 1522.

Lapulapu is remembered today as the first Filipino to resist foreign conquest. His statue stands in Mactan, and he appears on the 1-centavo coin.

THE LEGAZPI EXPEDITION AND PERMANENT SETTLEMENT (1565)

Spain sent several expeditions to the Philippines after Magellan's death, but none established a permanent foothold until Miguel Lopez de Legazpi arrived from Mexico in 1565. Legazpi established the first permanent Spanish settlement in Cebu. He brought with him Augustinian friar Andres de Urdaneta, who discovered the eastward return route across the Pacific — the tornaviaje — making regular trans-Pacific trade possible.

In 1571, Legazpi moved north and captured Manila from its Muslim ruler, Rajah Sulayman, after a brief battle. He established Manila as the colonial capital, a position it has held ever since. Manila's location at the mouth of the Pasig River, with its large natural bay, made it an ideal hub for trade.

THE MANILA GALLEON TRADE (1565-1815)

For 250 years, the Manila Galleon Trade connected the Philippines to Mexico and Spain. Ships loaded with Chinese silk, porcelain, spices, and other Asian goods sailed from Manila to Acapulco each year. On the return voyage, they brought silver — primarily from the mines of Mexico and Peru — which flowed into China to pay for the goods.

The trade made Manila one of the world's great commercial centers. It also shaped the Philippines' cultural makeup: a significant Chinese merchant community (the Sangley) became a permanent fixture of Manila, and Latin American influences entered Filipino culture through the Acapulco connection.

The galleon trade ended in 1815 when Mexico declared independence from Spain, cutting the trade route.

THE ENCOMIENDA SYSTEM AND FORCED LABOR

Spain governed the Philippines through a system of encomiendas — grants of territory and labor given to Spanish soldiers and colonists in exchange for military service. Encomenderos were authorized to collect tribute from Filipinos and were supposed to provide them with military protection and Christian instruction in return. In practice, the system was routinely abusive. Filipinos were forced to provide labor, food, and goods far beyond what the law allowed.

The polo y servicio system required Filipino men to provide 40 days of forced labor per year for colonial projects — building churches, roads, ships, and fortifications. This could be commuted by paying a fee (falla), but most Filipinos could not afford it.

THE FRIAR ORDERS

The Catholic Church was the most powerful institution in colonial Philippine society, and within the Church, the religious orders — Augustinians, Franciscans, Dominicans, Jesuits, and Recollects — held the real power at the local level. Parish priests (nearly all Spanish friars) controlled not just religious life but civil administration in their towns. They kept birth and death records, reported on residents to the colonial government, and shaped social norms.

The friar orders accumulated enormous landholdings over the centuries. By the 19th century, the friar estates around Manila comprised hundreds of thousands of acres of the most productive agricultural land, worked by Filipino tenant farmers who paid rent and were routinely subjected to rent increases and evictions.

The friars' power and the abuses that came with it became the central grievance of the Philippine reform and independence movements in the 19th century.

THE SECULAR-REGULAR CONTROVERSY

As early as the 17th century, Filipino priests (secular clergy) sought the right to administer parishes currently controlled by the Spanish friar orders (regular clergy). The friar orders resisted, arguing that Filipino priests were not yet capable of running parishes. In reality, parish control meant income and local power.

The conflict came to a head in the 1870s. In 1872, Filipino priests Mariano Gomez, Jose Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora (GomBurZa) were accused of involvement in the Cavite Mutiny — a small revolt by Filipino soldiers and workers at the Cavite arsenal — and executed by garrote on February 17, 1872. The evidence against them was thin. Their execution was widely seen as the Spanish colonial government eliminating Filipino voices who demanded equality.

Jose Rizal dedicated El Filibusterismo to the memory of Gomez, Burgos, and Zamora.

REVOLTS UNDER SPANISH RULE

Filipino resistance to Spanish rule was constant throughout the colonial period. Major revolts include:

- 1574: Limahong, a Chinese pirate, briefly occupies parts of Luzon but is repelled.
- 1587-1588: The Tondo Conspiracy, a plot by Philippine nobility to overthrow Spanish rule with Japanese support, is discovered and crushed.
- 1660-1661: The Maniago Revolt in Pampanga and the Malong Revolt in Pangasinan, both triggered by forced labor and tribute demands.
- 1744-1829: The Dagohoy Rebellion in Bohol — the longest rebellion in Philippine colonial history, lasting 85 years.
- 1807: The Ambaristo Revolt in Ilocos Norte, led by Pedro Mateo.

Despite these revolts, no uprising before the 1896 revolution came close to dislodging Spanish rule.

END OF SPANISH RULE

By the late 19th century, Spain's hold on the Philippines was weakening. The reform movement led by Rizal and others had created a educated Filipino middle class — the ilustrado — who could articulate the injustices of colonial rule in terms Spain's own Enlightenment values. The Katipunan turned that articulation into armed rebellion. And when the United States declared war on Spain in 1898 over events in Cuba, the Philippines became a casualty of a war that was not really about the Philippines at all.

Spain's 333-year colonial rule ended not with a Filipino military victory but with a diplomatic transaction: the Treaty of Paris, signed by Spain and the United States on December 10, 1898, transferred sovereignty over the Philippines for $20 million. Filipinos were not present at the negotiating table.
