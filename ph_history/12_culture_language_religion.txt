PHILIPPINE CULTURE, LANGUAGE, AND RELIGION
============================================

LANGUAGES

The Philippines is one of the most linguistically diverse countries in the world. There are approximately 130 to 187 languages spoken across the archipelago, depending on how dialects are classified. The national language is Filipino, based primarily on Tagalog and officially designated by the 1987 constitution alongside English as a co-official language.

The major regional languages include:

- Tagalog: spoken natively in Metro Manila, Bulacan, Batangas, Cavite, Laguna, Quezon, and Rizal. The basis of the national language, Filipino.
- Cebuano (Bisaya): the most widely spoken native language by number of first-language speakers, used throughout the Visayas and much of Mindanao.
- Ilocano: spoken in the Ilocos Region, parts of Cagayan Valley, and Cordillera.
- Hiligaynon (Ilonggo): spoken in Iloilo, Negros Occidental, and parts of Mindanao.
- Bicol: spoken in the Bicol Peninsula of southern Luzon.
- Waray: spoken in Leyte and Samar.
- Kapampangan: spoken in Pampanga and Tarlac, one of the oldest Philippine literary languages.
- Chavacano: a Spanish-based creole spoken in Zamboanga City, the only Spanish creole language in Asia.

English is used widely in government, education, law, business, and media. The Philippines is one of the largest English-speaking countries in the world by population, a legacy of American colonial education.

RELIGION

The Philippines is the only predominantly Christian country in Asia. Approximately 80 percent of Filipinos are Roman Catholic, a legacy of 333 years of Spanish colonial rule. An additional 8 percent belong to other Christian denominations, including the Philippine Independent Church (Aglipayan Church), founded in 1902 as a break from Rome.

The Iglesia ni Cristo (Church of Christ), founded in 1914 by Felix Manalo, is a uniquely Filipino Christian denomination with a significant and politically influential membership.

Approximately 5 to 6 percent of Filipinos are Muslim, concentrated in the Bangsamoro region of Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago. Philippine Muslims, known as Moros, have maintained distinct cultural and political identities through centuries of resistance to Spanish colonization, American rule, and the Manila-based central government.

Indigenous belief systems persist among highland and tribal communities, including the Igorots of the Cordillera, the Lumad of Mindanao, and the Mangyan of Mindoro.

CORE CULTURAL VALUES

Filipino culture is shaped by a complex mix of indigenous, Malay, Chinese, Spanish, and American influences. Several values are widely recognized as central to Filipino identity:

- Bayanihan: the spirit of communal unity and cooperation. The word comes from the practice of neighbors helping each other move a house — literally lifting it on bamboo poles and carrying it together. It represents the idea that the community helps one member in need.
- Utang na loob: a deep sense of gratitude and reciprocal obligation. Receiving help from someone creates a debt that must be repaid. This value structures relationships from family to politics.
- Hiya: a sense of shame that regulates social behavior. Acting in ways that would bring shame to oneself or one's family is a powerful social restraint.
- Pakikisama: the value of getting along, maintaining group harmony, and not breaking ranks. It can discourage dissent and honest criticism but also builds social cohesion.
- Pagmamalasakit: caring deeply for others' welfare, especially within one's family and community.

FAMILY

Family is the central institution of Filipino social life. The Filipino family typically extends beyond the nuclear unit to include grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins as close kin with real obligations. The ninong and ninang system — godparents in baptism and marriage — extends the family network further, creating bonds of ritual kinship that carry social obligations.

The balikbayan phenomenon — Filipinos who go abroad for work and return with gifts — reflects both the importance of family bonds and the reality that an estimated 10 million Filipinos live and work overseas as Overseas Filipino Workers (OFWs), sending remittances that make up a significant portion of the Philippine GDP.

FESTIVALS

Filipino culture has a rich tradition of festivals (fiestas), rooted in Catholic observance but incorporating pre-colonial celebration:

- Sinulog (Cebu): held in January, honoring the Santo Nino. One of the largest festivals in the Philippines, featuring elaborate street dancing in bright costumes.
- Ati-Atihan (Aklan): the oldest festival in the Philippines, also honoring the Santo Nino, known as the "mother of all Philippine festivals."
- Panagbenga (Baguio): a flower festival held in February, featuring floats decorated with fresh flowers.
- Moriones (Marinduque): a Holy Week festival in which participants dress as Roman soldiers in commemoration of the legend of Longinus.

FOOD

Filipino cuisine reflects its cultural history. Spanish influence brought adobo (meat braised in vinegar, soy sauce, and garlic), lechon (roasted pig), and kare-kare (oxtail stew with peanut sauce). Chinese influence appears in pancit (noodle dishes) and lumpia (spring rolls). American influence introduced fast food culture that has deeply penetrated Filipino eating habits.

Distinctively Filipino flavors tend toward sourness (sinigang, a tamarind-based sour soup is arguably the national dish alongside adobo) and the combination of sweet, salty, and sour in the same dish. Rice is served at every meal, including breakfast.

ART AND LITERATURE

Philippine literature predates Spanish arrival. The Ibalon, the Biag ni Lam-ang, and other epics from various ethnic groups represent pre-colonial literary traditions. The Spanish period produced religious poetry and drama. The 19th century saw Rizal, del Pilar, and others writing in Spanish as instruments of nationalist critique.

The 20th century produced significant literature in English — Nick Joaquin, F. Sionil Jose, and others — and an emerging body of work in Filipino. The national artist system, established in 1972, recognizes Filipinos who have made outstanding contributions to the arts. Notable national artists include painter Fernando Amorsolo, sculptor Guillermo Tolentino, writer Nick Joaquin, and filmmaker Lino Brocka.
