PHILIPPINE-AMERICAN RELATIONS
==============================

ORIGINS: IMPERIALISM AND THE ANTI-IMPERIALIST DEBATE

The United States acquired the Philippines from Spain in 1898 under the Treaty of Paris for $20 million — a transaction negotiated without any Filipino participation. The acquisition sparked a serious debate within the United States. The Anti-Imperialist League, which included Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, and former President Grover Cleveland, argued that subjugating the Philippines violated American principles of self-government and democracy.

Imperialist advocates, including Senator Albert Beveridge and President McKinley, argued that the United States had a duty to civilize and uplift the Filipino people — a rationale barely distinguishable from European colonial justifications. McKinley reportedly told a group of ministers that God had told him the Philippines must be kept, educated, and Christianized, apparently unaware that the Philippines was already predominantly Catholic.

The Philippine-American War (1899-1902) killed far more Filipinos than Spanish colonization had in the preceding decades. American atrocities — documented at the time, suppressed later — included the water cure torture, the massacre at Bud Dajo, and General Smith's order to turn Samar into a howling wilderness. The war cost the United States about $600 million and 4,200 dead, vastly more than the $20 million purchase price.

THE COLONIAL RELATIONSHIP (1902-1946)

American governance of the Philippines combined genuine reform efforts with the self-interest of a colonial power. The public education system, expanded under American policy, gave the Philippines a literate, English-speaking population — a legacy that continues to shape the country's economy through its service industries and overseas workforce.

The Philippine Commission and later the Resident Commissioners to the United States Congress gave Filipinos limited representation. Genuine autonomy, however, was always constrained. The Jones Act of 1916 promised eventual independence. The Hare-Hawes-Cutting Act of 1932 and the Tydings-McDuffie Act of 1934 set the transition timeline at ten years. Both reflected American strategic and economic interests as much as Philippine aspirations: the United States sugar industry wanted Philippine competition limited, and the American labor movement wanted Filipino immigration restricted.

The Commonwealth period (1935-1941) gave Filipinos substantial domestic self-governance while the United States retained control of defense and foreign policy. The relationship was paternalistic but less openly coercive than direct colonial rule.

WORLD WAR II AND THE ALLIANCE

The Japanese invasion of 1941 and the shared fight to liberate the Philippines created a new dimension in Philippine-American relations: military alliance born from shared sacrifice. The Fil-American forces that fought and died in Bataan — both Filipino soldiers and American soldiers — created a bond that shaped Philippine foreign policy for decades.

MacArthur's return in 1944 and the liberation of 1944-1945 were experienced by most Filipinos as liberation, despite the enormous destruction the Battle of Manila entailed. The United States was seen as an ally and liberator, not merely a colonial power.

American recognition of Philippine independence on July 4, 1946, was genuine, though the Bell Trade Act attached economic conditions that constrained full sovereignty.

THE BASES AGREEMENT AND COLD WAR ALLIANCE

After independence, the United States maintained large military bases in the Philippines. Clark Air Base in Pampanga was the largest American air base outside the continental United States. Subic Bay Naval Base in Zambales was the largest American naval installation in Asia. Together, they represented the backbone of American military presence in the western Pacific during the Cold War.

The Mutual Defense Treaty of 1951 committed the United States to defend the Philippines in case of attack. Philippine troops fought alongside American forces in the Korean War and later in Vietnam.

The bases were sources of economic activity for surrounding communities — thousands of Filipinos were employed on base, and the surrounding towns of Olongapo and Angeles developed economies dependent on American military personnel and their spending. They were also sources of significant social problems, including prostitution, environmental contamination, and jurisdictional disputes over crimes committed by American military personnel.

MARCOS, AQUINO, AND THE BASES QUESTION

The Marcos regime was sustained in part by American support. The United States continued military and economic assistance to Marcos through most of his dictatorship, prioritizing the bases and Cold War alliance over human rights concerns. This support did not end until the mid-1980s, when Marcos's political collapse made him a liability.

After the People Power Revolution, Corazon Aquino negotiated a new Bases Agreement. When it came to the Senate for ratification in 1991, the Senate rejected it, 12-11. Combined with the catastrophic eruption of Mount Pinatubo, which had already severely damaged Clark Air Base, the vote ended the American military presence in the Philippines. American forces left Clark in 1991 and Subic in 1992.

MODERN ALLIANCE: THE VISITING FORCES AGREEMENT AND BEYOND

After a period of reduced military ties, the alliance was rebuilt through the Visiting Forces Agreement (VFA) of 1998, which allowed American troops to return to the Philippines for joint exercises and training. The Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement (EDCA) of 2014 went further, allowing the United States to pre-position military equipment and use Philippine bases on a rotational basis.

Relations have fluctuated with Philippine administrations. Rodrigo Duterte threatened to cancel the VFA in 2020 before reversing course in 2021. Ferdinand Marcos Jr. has pursued closer military ties with the United States, expanding EDCA access to additional Philippine bases.

The South China Sea territorial dispute — in which the Philippines, China, and several other nations claim overlapping waters and features — has become a central issue in the relationship. The 2016 arbitration ruling that voided China's expansive claims was hailed by the United States, which is a treaty ally of the Philippines. China ignored the ruling. How to manage this dispute while maintaining economic ties with China is the central foreign policy challenge facing the Philippines.

FILIPINO AMERICANS

The Filipino-American community is one of the largest Asian-American groups in the United States, numbering approximately 4 million. Filipinos began arriving in the United States in significant numbers in the early 20th century, many as agricultural laborers in Hawaii and California. The 1965 Immigration Act opened the door to large-scale immigration from the Philippines, particularly of nurses, doctors, engineers, and other professionals.

Filipino Americans are heavily represented in American healthcare: an estimated 150,000 to 200,000 Filipino nurses work in the United States, making Filipinos the largest foreign-born group in the American nursing workforce.
