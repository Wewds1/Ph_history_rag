THE PHILIPPINES IN WORLD WAR II (1941-1945)
============================================

THE JAPANESE ATTACK

On December 8, 1941 — one day after the attack on Pearl Harbor — Japanese air forces bombed Clark Air Base and Iba Field, destroying most of the American Far East Air Force on the ground. The Philippines was strategically vital: it sat across Japan's sea lanes to the resource-rich Dutch East Indies and Malaya.

General Douglas MacArthur commanded the combined American and Filipino forces (USAFFE — United States Armed Forces in the Far East). Despite advance warning that war was coming, the destruction of the air force in the opening hours left the defense of the Philippines critically weakened.

Japanese ground forces landed in northern Luzon in December 1941 and drove south toward Manila. MacArthur declared Manila an "open city" to spare it from destruction and ordered his forces to retreat to the Bataan Peninsula.

THE FALL OF BATAAN (APRIL 9, 1942)

For four months, about 80,000 American and Filipino soldiers — already short on food, medicine, and ammunition — fought the Japanese in the jungles and mountains of Bataan. The fighting was brutal on both sides.

By early April 1942, the troops were starving and riddled with malaria, dysentery, and other diseases. Major General Edward King surrendered the Bataan forces on April 9, 1942. It was the largest surrender of American-led forces in history.

President Quezon and MacArthur had already been ordered to evacuate. MacArthur left Bataan by PT boat in March, eventually reaching Australia where he made his famous pledge: "I shall return."

THE BATAAN DEATH MARCH

After the surrender, approximately 76,000 Filipino and American prisoners of war were forced to march roughly 100 kilometers (about 65 miles) from Mariveles, Bataan, to Camp O'Donnell in Tarlac under the Japanese guards' supervision.

The march lasted six days to two weeks. Prisoners received almost no food or water. Those who fell were beaten, bayoneted, or shot. Estimates of deaths during the march range from 5,000 to 18,000 Filipino soldiers and 500 to 650 American soldiers. Thousands more died in Camp O'Donnell in the months that followed.

The Bataan Death March is one of the most documented war crimes of the Pacific War. General Masaharu Homma, the Japanese commander responsible, was tried and executed after the war.

JAPANESE OCCUPATION (1942-1945)

Japan established a military occupation government and later a nominally independent Philippine Executive Commission, which became the Second Philippine Republic in 1943 under Jose P. Laurel. Most Filipino political leaders who cooperated with Japan did so reluctantly and many maintained secret contacts with resistance forces.

Resistance to Japanese occupation was widespread. Guerrilla units operated throughout the archipelago, harassing Japanese forces and providing intelligence to MacArthur's command in Australia. The Hukbalahap (People's Army Against the Japanese), led by Luis Taruc, was particularly active in Central Luzon.

Filipino guerrillas faced brutal reprisals: villages were burned, civilians massacred, and resistance fighters executed when captured.

LEYTE AND MACARTHUR'S RETURN (1944)

On October 20, 1944, Allied forces landed at Leyte Gulf. MacArthur waded ashore and broadcast to the Filipino people: "People of the Philippines, I have returned."

The Battle of Leyte Gulf (October 23-26, 1944) was the largest naval battle in history, involving over 200 vessels on both sides. The Japanese fleet suffered catastrophic losses and never recovered as an offensive force.

THE BATTLE OF MANILA (FEBRUARY-MARCH 1945)

As American forces drove toward Manila, the Japanese commander, Admiral Sanji Iwabuchi, ordered his troops to defend the city rather than withdraw. Over a month of urban combat reduced much of Manila to rubble. The Japanese committed systematic massacres of civilians — burning hospitals with patients inside, executing men and raping women in neighborhoods across the city.

An estimated 100,000 Filipino civilians died in the Battle of Manila. The destruction was comparable to Warsaw. It was the most heavily damaged Allied capital city of World War II after Warsaw.

Manila was liberated in early March 1945, but the price was catastrophic. Throughout the Philippines, total Filipino deaths from the war are estimated at one million.

LIBERATION AND INDEPENDENCE

Formal Japanese surrender in the Philippines occurred on September 3, 1945, days after the general surrender aboard the USS Missouri. The Philippines had been devastated: its capital in ruins, its economy destroyed, its people exhausted and traumatized.

On July 4, 1946, the United States granted the Philippines full independence as promised. The American flag was lowered at Luneta and the Philippine flag was raised. Manuel Roxas, elected president in April 1946, became the first president of the independent Third Republic of the Philippines.

The date July 4 was changed to June 12 (Independence Day) in 1962 under President Diosdado Macapagal, to align the holiday with Aguinaldo's 1898 proclamation rather than the American handover.
