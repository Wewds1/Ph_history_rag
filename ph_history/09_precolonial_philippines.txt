PRE-COLONIAL PHILIPPINES
=========================

EARLIEST INHABITANTS

The Philippine archipelago has been inhabited for an extraordinarily long time. Stone tools discovered in the Cagayan Valley in northern Luzon suggest hominin presence at least 709,000 years ago. In 2019, fossils from Callao Cave in Cagayan were identified as belonging to a previously unknown human species named Homo luzonensis, with an estimated age of at least 134,000 years.

The Tabon Caves in Palawan have yielded remains of anatomically modern humans dating to approximately 47,000 years ago — the Tabon Man. These early Filipinos lived alongside now-extinct megafauna and made stone tools found in the cave sediments.

The Negritos — among them the Aeta, Ati, and Agta peoples — are considered among the earliest settlers. They are believed to have arrived from mainland Southeast Asia via land bridges that existed during the ice ages, when sea levels were much lower.

AUSTRONESIAN MIGRATIONS

Beginning around 3000 BCE, a new wave of people arrived in the Philippines from Taiwan: the Austronesians. These seafaring peoples were skilled in agriculture, pottery, and navigation. They brought with them rice cultivation, weaving, and a social organization based on kinship groups called barangays. Over several thousand years, Austronesians spread from Taiwan through the Philippines and outward to Madagascar in the west and Polynesia in the east — one of the most remarkable human migrations in history.

The word barangay originally referred to the large outrigger boats in which these groups arrived. It came to mean the community itself — a kinship group of 30 to 100 households led by a datu (chief). The barangay remains the basic unit of Philippine local government today.

SOCIAL STRUCTURE

Pre-colonial Filipino society was organized into barangays, typically with 30 to 100 households. The social hierarchy had three main classes:

- Datu (nobility): chiefs who held political authority, controlled land, and led in war. The title was hereditary.
- Timawa (freemen): free people who owed loyalty and military service to their datu but were not enslaved. In Visayan society, timawa held a distinct warrior status.
- Alipin (dependent class): people in various forms of debt bondage. The alipin system was not chattel slavery in the Western sense — people could buy their way out of bondage, and alipin could own property. But it was a system of coerced labor and subordination.

TRADE AND LITERACY

Pre-colonial Filipinos were active traders long before Spanish arrival. The Philippines had established trade relationships with China, Java, Borneo, Siam (Thailand), and India by at least the 10th century. Chinese trade goods — porcelain, silk, bronze — appear throughout Philippine archaeological sites.

The Laguna Copperplate Inscription, dated 900 CE, is the oldest known written document found in the Philippines. Written in a mix of Old Malay, Old Javanese, and Old Tagalog using Kawi script, it records the settlement of a debt. Its existence proves that writing was already practiced in the Philippines more than 600 years before Spanish arrival.

Filipinos had their own writing system, called Baybayin (sometimes called Alibata), an abugida script related to scripts found in Sulawesi and Sumatra. Baybayin was written on perishable materials — bamboo, leaves, bark — which is why so few pre-colonial documents survive. Spanish missionaries documented and used Baybayin in early catechisms and religious texts, but colonial education systematically replaced it with the Latin alphabet.

EARLY POLITIES

By the time of Spanish arrival, the Philippines was not a single political entity but a collection of hundreds of independent polities ranging from small barangays to more complex states:

- The Kingdom of Tondo, based at the mouth of the Pasig River in what is now Manila, was one of the oldest and most powerful polities in Luzon. It had close ties to Majapahit (the great Hindu-Buddhist empire of Java) and later to the Sultanate of Brunei.
- The Sultanate of Sulu, established in the late 14th century after the arrival of Islamic missionaries, became the dominant power in the southwestern Philippines and a major force in regional trade.
- The Sultanate of Maguindanao in Mindanao controlled much of the large southern island.
- Rajahnate of Cebu, ruled by Rajah Humabon at the time of Magellan's arrival, was a significant trading center in the Visayas.
- Rajahnate of Butuan in northeastern Mindanao was an early gold-trading polity with connections to China and Java.

RELIGION BEFORE SPANISH ARRIVAL

The religious landscape of pre-colonial Philippines was diverse. Islam had taken hold in the Sulu archipelago by the late 14th century and was spreading northward through Mindanao and into Luzon by the 16th century. Had Spain arrived a century later, Islam might have become dominant across the archipelago.

The non-Islamized majority of Filipinos practiced animism — a belief system in which spirits (anito) inhabit natural features like mountains, rivers, trees, and the sea. Rituals were led by babaylan, spiritual practitioners who were often women, who mediated between the human and spirit worlds. These practices were not a unified religion but a diverse set of local traditions.

Elements of Hindu-Buddhist influence, likely transmitted through Javanese and Malay contact, can be detected in the cosmology, social structure, and material culture of some Philippine groups.

WHAT THE SPANISH FOUND — AND ERASED

The Spanish encountered a literate, trading, socially organized society that was deeply connected to the rest of maritime Southeast Asia. Colonial accounts that described Filipinos as primitive or without culture reflected the ideology of colonial justification, not archaeological or historical reality.

Much of pre-colonial Filipino culture was deliberately suppressed. Baybayin was replaced by the Latin alphabet. Local religious practices were condemned as devil worship. Oral traditions were disrupted. Because pre-colonial Filipinos used perishable writing materials, the physical record is thin, and what survived was largely filtered through colonial documentation.

Recovering pre-colonial Philippine history is an ongoing project in Philippine scholarship.
