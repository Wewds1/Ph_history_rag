THE AMERICAN COLONIAL PERIOD AND THE COMMONWEALTH (1898-1941)
==============================================================

THE PHILIPPINE-AMERICAN WAR (1899-1902)

The Philippine-American War began on February 4, 1899, when American and Filipino soldiers exchanged fire near the San Juan Bridge in Manila. Private Willie Grayson fired what is generally considered the first shot.

The war lasted officially until 1902, though fighting in Mindanao and Samar continued for years afterward. The conflict was brutal on both sides. The United States employed tactics including the "water cure" (a form of torture), the concentration of civilians into "reconcentration zones," and the scorched-earth pacification of Samar island following the Balangiga Massacre of 1901, in which Filipino guerrillas killed 48 American soldiers. General Jacob Smith ordered his men to turn Samar into "a howling wilderness" and to kill everyone capable of bearing arms over the age of ten.

Filipino casualties from combat, disease, and famine during the war are estimated between 200,000 and 1,000,000. American deaths numbered around 4,200.

Emilio Aguinaldo was captured on March 23, 1901, by General Frederick Funston using a daring deception — Funston posed as a prisoner, escorted by Filipino scouts posing as Aguinaldo's reinforcements. Aguinaldo swore allegiance to the United States and called on his followers to lay down their arms.

THE BENEVOLENT ASSIMILATION POLICY

President William McKinley described American intentions in the Philippines as "benevolent assimilation" — a policy of governing the islands for the Filipinos' own good, preparing them gradually for self-government. Critics, including American anti-imperialists like Mark Twain, pointed out the contradiction: the United States had just fought a war to liberate Cuba from Spain and was now fighting a war to subject Filipinos to American rule.

The Philippine Commission, first chaired by William Howard Taft, governed the islands. Taft — who called Filipinos "our little brown brothers" — pursued policies of building infrastructure, expanding public education, and integrating the Filipino elite into colonial governance.

EDUCATION: THE THOMASITES

One of the most significant early American policies was the rapid expansion of free public education in English. In 1901, the United States sent 540 American teachers to the Philippines aboard the transport ship USS Thomas. These teachers became known as the Thomasites.

Their mission was to establish a public school system and teach in English. Filipino teachers were trained rapidly to replace them. By 1927, Filipino teachers made up nearly all of the 26,200 teachers in Philippine public schools. By the 1930s, literacy had roughly doubled. English became the language of government, law, education, and commerce — a legacy that persists today.

THE JONES ACT (1916)

The Philippine Autonomy Act, commonly known as the Jones Act of 1916, reorganized the Philippine government by replacing the appointed Philippine Commission with an elected Philippine Senate. It also declared that it was the intention of the United States to grant independence to the Philippines once a stable government had been established.

The Jones Act significantly expanded Filipino participation in their own government, though ultimate authority remained with the American Governor-General.

THE TYDINGS-MCDUFFIE ACT AND THE COMMONWEALTH (1934-1935)

The Tydings-McDuffie Act of 1934 established a ten-year transition period to full Philippine independence. During this period, the Philippines would be governed as a Commonwealth — largely self-governing, but with the United States retaining authority over defense and foreign policy.

Elections were held in 1935. Manuel L. Quezon, the dominant Filipino political figure of the era, was elected the first President of the Commonwealth of the Philippines by a wide margin. Sergio Osmena was elected Vice President. Quezon served until 1944, including a period of government-in-exile in the United States after the Japanese invasion.

QUEZON AND THE LANGUAGE QUESTION

One of Quezon's notable acts was addressing the question of a national language. The Philippines had over 80 languages and no common tongue aside from Spanish (spoken mainly by the elite) and English (used in government and education). In 1937, the National Language Institute recommended Tagalog as the basis for a national language. Quezon signed an executive order making Tagalog the foundation of Filipino — the national language. Full implementation took decades, but the decision shaped modern Philippine identity.

THE EVE OF WORLD WAR II

By 1941, the Philippines was on track for full independence in 1946. General Douglas MacArthur, who had retired from the American army, had been recalled to active service as commander of US Army Forces in the Far East (USAFFE), in anticipation of possible conflict with Japan.

The Philippine economy had become closely tied to the United States through preferential trade arrangements. Sugar, coconut oil, and other agricultural exports went to American markets duty-free, creating a dependent economic relationship that independence alone would not easily break.

When Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941 (December 8 in the Philippines), the colonial relationship between the Philippines and the United States was about to be tested in the most brutal way possible.
