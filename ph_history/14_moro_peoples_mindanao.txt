THE MORO PEOPLES AND THE MINDANAO CONFLICT
==========================================

WHO ARE THE MOROS?

The term "Moro" was applied by Spanish colonizers to the Muslim peoples of Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago, derived from "Moor" — the Spanish term for North African Muslims. Though originally pejorative, Filipino Muslims have reclaimed the term as a marker of identity and resistance.

The Moro peoples are not a single ethnic group but a collection of at least thirteen distinct ethnolinguistic groups, the largest of which are:

- Maguindanao: the largest Moro group, centered in the Cotabato Basin of central Mindanao. Their sultanate, the Sultanate of Maguindanao, was one of the most powerful Muslim polities in pre-colonial Southeast Asia.
- Maranao: centered around Lake Lanao in Lanao del Sur. Known for their elaborate artistic traditions, particularly the torogan (traditional royal house) and the okir design motif.
- Tausug: the dominant group in the Sulu Archipelago, centered on Jolo island. The Sultanate of Sulu, established in 1457, was a major regional maritime power.
- Iranun and Sama-Bajau: maritime peoples of the Sulu Sea and Celebes Sea, historically engaged in trade and, during certain periods, in slave-raiding across the region.

RESISTANCE TO SPANISH COLONIZATION

The Spanish never fully conquered Mindanao or Sulu. From 1565 through the end of Spanish rule, the Moros resisted Spanish military campaigns in a series of conflicts the Spanish called the Moro Wars. Spanish forces made repeated expeditions to Mindanao, attacking Moro settlements and forts. The Moros responded with raids on Christianized Philippine communities in the Visayas and Luzon, taking captives.

The conflict had the character of a long, slow stalemate. The Spanish held the coast in limited areas but never controlled the interior of Mindanao or the Sulu Archipelago in any meaningful sense.

AMERICAN PERIOD: PACIFICATION AND LAND POLICY

The United States was more aggressive in attempting to extend control over Mindanao. The Bates Treaty of 1899, which promised to respect the authority of the Sultan of Sulu, was unilaterally abrogated in 1904. American forces fought several significant campaigns against Moro resistance, most notoriously the Battle of Bud Dajo in 1906, where American forces killed between 600 and 900 Moro men, women, and children who had taken refuge in the crater of an extinct volcano.

American land policy in Mindanao opened the region to Christian Filipino migration from Luzon and the Visayas. Under the concept of "public land" — which did not recognize indigenous or Moro customary land tenure — the American colonial government encouraged resettlement. This demographic shift continued through the Commonwealth period and after independence, transforming Mindanao from a region with a Moro majority into one where Christians eventually outnumbered Muslims.

POST-INDEPENDENCE: MIGRATION AND MARGINALIZATION

The migration of Christian Filipinos into Mindanao accelerated rapidly after independence, encouraged by government resettlement programs under Quezon and Magsaysay. Settlers were granted homestead rights to land that Moros considered their ancestral territory.

By the 1960s, Moro communities were facing displacement from their lands, political marginalization in a government dominated by Christian Filipinos, and cultural suppression. Land conflicts between settlers and Moros became increasingly violent.

THE MORO NATIONAL LIBERATION FRONT (MNLF)

The Moro National Liberation Front was established in 1969 by Nur Misuari, a political science professor at the University of the Philippines. Its goal was an independent Bangsamoro state — "Bangsa Moro" meaning "Moro Nation" — in Mindanao, Sulu, and Palawan.

The MNLF launched an armed insurgency in 1972, coinciding with Marcos's declaration of Martial Law. The conflict was brutal. An estimated 100,000 to 120,000 people died in the fighting between 1972 and 1976. Hundreds of thousands were displaced.

Libya and Malaysia mediated negotiations. The Tripoli Agreement of 1976 promised regional autonomy for 13 provinces in Mindanao, but implementation stalled. The MNLF signed a final peace agreement with the Philippine government in 1996, and Nur Misuari was appointed governor of the Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (ARMM). But the agreement satisfied neither all Moro groups nor all Manila politicians.

THE MORO ISLAMIC LIBERATION FRONT (MILF)

The MILF broke away from the MNLF in 1977 under Salamat Hashim, dissatisfied with the Tripoli Agreement's autonomy framework and seeking an Islamic state rather than mere regional autonomy. It became the larger and more powerful of the two major Moro armed groups.

The MILF controlled significant territory in central Mindanao and fought the Philippine military through the 1990s and 2000s. A Memorandum of Agreement on Ancestral Domain reached in 2008 was struck down by the Philippine Supreme Court, reigniting violence.

THE BANGSAMORO ORGANIC LAW (2018) AND BARMM (2019)

After years of negotiations facilitated by Malaysia and international mediators, the Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro was signed in 2014 between the Philippine government and the MILF. The agreement established a new autonomous political entity — the Bangsamoro — to replace the ARMM.

The Bangsamoro Organic Law was ratified by Congress in 2018 and approved in a plebiscite by Bangsamoro voters in January 2019. The Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) was formally established in February 2019 with an interim government. The BARMM has its own parliament, expanded fiscal autonomy, and broader powers than the ARMM it replaced. The MILF's political wing, the United Bangsamoro Justice Party, controls the interim government.

The BARMM represents the most significant step toward political resolution of the Mindanao conflict in Philippine history, though armed splinter groups — including the Abu Sayyaf and remnants of the Bangsamoro Islamic Freedom Fighters — continue to operate.
