THE KATIPUNAN AND THE PHILIPPINE REVOLUTION (1892-1898)
=========================================================

THE KATIPUNAN (KKK)

The Kataastaasang Kagalanggalangang Katipunan ng mga Anak ng Bayan — usually shortened to Katipunan, or KKK — was a secret revolutionary society founded in Manila in July 1892 by Andres Bonifacio and a small group of companions, shortly after Jose Rizal's arrest and exile.

Where Rizal's La Liga Filipina sought peaceful reform, the Katipunan had one goal: independence from Spain through armed revolution. New members swore an oath of loyalty written in their own blood. The society spread rapidly through the working-class districts of Manila and into neighboring provinces. By August 1896, membership had grown to an estimated 100,000.

ANDRES BONIFACIO

Andres Bonifacio was born in Tondo, Manila, in 1863, to a working-class family. He was largely self-educated — he could not attend elite institutions as Rizal could — but he read voraciously, including the works of the French Revolution, Victor Hugo, and Jose Rizal himself. He worked as a warehouse clerk and street vendor before founding the Katipunan.

Bonifacio represented a different current in Filipino nationalism: not the educated ilustrado (elite) class, but the common people. His approach was direct — Spain would not reform willingly, so it had to be expelled by force. He remains a contested figure in Philippine history because of how his story ended, at the hands of his revolutionary successor.

THE CRY OF PUGAD LAWIN (1896)

In August 1896, the Spanish authorities uncovered evidence of the Katipunan's existence. Facing exposure, Bonifacio called his followers to action. On August 23, 1896, at Pugad Lawin (sometimes called Balintawak), Bonifacio and his Katipuneros tore up their cedulas — the residence certificates that symbolized their subjugation to Spanish authority — and shouted "Long live the Philippines!" This act is commemorated as the start of the Philippine Revolution.

The first engagements went badly for the revolutionaries. Outgunned and lacking military experience, they lost most early battles in Luzon. But in Cavite province, a different leader emerged.

EMILIO AGUINALDO AND THE CAVITE CAMPAIGNS

Emilio Aguinaldo, the mayor of Kawit, Cavite, proved a more effective military commander than Bonifacio. His forces won a series of battles in Cavite, controlling much of the province by late 1896. His successes gave him political leverage that Bonifacio, despite being the Katipunan's founder, could not match.

THE TEJEROS CONVENTION (1897) AND THE DEATH OF BONIFACIO

In March 1897, the leaders of the revolution met at the Tejeros Convention to form a new government. Aguinaldo was elected president. Bonifacio was elected to a cabinet post but the results were challenged. In the chaos that followed, Bonifacio was stripped of his position.

Bonifacio refused to accept the convention's legitimacy and attempted to form a parallel government. He was arrested on Aguinaldo's orders, tried for treason and sedition, and executed on May 10, 1897. His death remains one of the most controversial episodes in Philippine history: the founder of the revolution, killed by the revolution he started.

THE PACT OF BIAK-NA-BATO (1897)

By late 1897, the revolution had stalemated. Spain could not crush the Katipunan entirely; the Katipunan could not drive Spain out. In December 1897, both sides agreed to the Pact of Biak-na-Bato: Aguinaldo and the revolutionary leadership would go into exile in Hong Kong in exchange for amnesty and a cash payment of 800,000 pesos. Promised reforms — representation in the Spanish Cortes, expulsion of abusive friars, legal equality — were never implemented.

Aguinaldo used the money to buy weapons.

THE SPANISH-AMERICAN WAR AND INDEPENDENCE (1898)

On April 25, 1898, war broke out between the United States and Spain, triggered by events in Cuba. On May 1, the American fleet under Commodore George Dewey destroyed the Spanish naval squadron in Manila Bay in a matter of hours. Spain's presence in the Pacific suddenly looked very fragile.

American officials encouraged Aguinaldo to return to the Philippines. He did so in May, landing from an American warship. He resumed the revolution and Filipino forces quickly seized most of Luzon from Spanish control.

On June 12, 1898, Aguinaldo proclaimed Philippine independence at his home in Kawit, Cavite. The national anthem was played; the Philippine flag — designed by Aguinaldo's wife and commissioned in Hong Kong — was unfurled. This date is now celebrated as Philippine Independence Day.

On August 13, 1898, Manila fell — to American forces, in a staged battle with the Spanish that deliberately excluded Filipino troops. Spain and America had already been negotiating a surrender. The Filipinos, who had done much of the work of the revolution, were not invited to the peace talks.

The Treaty of Paris, signed on December 10, 1898, transferred the Philippines from Spain to the United States for $20 million. The Filipinos were not consulted.

THE MALOLOS CONGRESS AND THE FIRST REPUBLIC (1899)

The Filipinos responded by convening the Malolos Congress and drafting a constitution. On January 23, 1899, the First Philippine Republic was inaugurated in Malolos, Bulacan, with Aguinaldo as president. It was the first constitutional republic in Asia.

Eleven days later, on February 4, 1899, fighting broke out between American and Filipino soldiers near Manila. The Philippine-American War had begun.
