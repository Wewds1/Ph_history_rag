FERDINAND MARCOS AND MARTIAL LAW (1965-1986)
=============================================

EARLY CAREER

Ferdinand Emmanuel Edralin Marcos was born on September 11, 1917, in Sarrat, Ilocos Norte. He studied law at the University of the Philippines and passed the bar in 1939 with the highest score in Philippine history. He was a controversial figure from the start: in 1939, he was convicted of murdering a political rival of his father but got his conviction overturned on appeal — a case he argued himself before the Supreme Court.

During World War II, Marcos claimed to have led a guerrilla unit called Ang Mga Maharlika and accumulated one of the most decorated military records of any Filipino soldier. After the war, these claims were investigated by American authorities and found to be largely or entirely fabricated. The wartime hero narrative nonetheless became central to his political identity.

He served in the House of Representatives and the Senate, eventually becoming Senate President. He was elected President of the Philippines in 1965, defeating incumbent Diosdado Macapagal.

THE FIRST AND SECOND TERMS (1965-1972)

Marcos's first term saw significant infrastructure development — roads, bridges, irrigation systems. He was re-elected in 1969, becoming the first Philippine president to win a second term. His re-election campaign was widely reported to have involved massive vote buying funded by government money.

By the early 1970s, the Philippines was in turmoil. The Communist Party of the Philippines, founded in 1968, had launched an armed insurgency through its military arm, the New People's Army. The Moro National Liberation Front was fighting for Muslim autonomy in Mindanao. Student protests and urban unrest were spreading.

Marcos faced a constitutional barrier: he could not seek a third term. The constitution limited presidents to two terms.

DECLARATION OF MARTIAL LAW (SEPTEMBER 21, 1972)

On September 21, 1972, Marcos signed Proclamation 1081, declaring Martial Law across the Philippines. He cited the communist insurgency and the Moro rebellion as justification. Critics then and historians since have argued that Marcos manufactured or exaggerated the crises to provide cover for eliminating term limits and seizing permanent power.

Key events used to justify Martial Law included a grenade attack on a political rally and an alleged assassination attempt on Defense Secretary Juan Ponce Enrile — an event Enrile himself later admitted was staged.

Under Martial Law, Marcos:

- Suspended the constitution and dissolved Congress
- Arrested thousands of political opponents, journalists, and activists, including Senator Benigno Aquino Jr.
- Shut down independent newspapers, television stations, and radio stations
- Abolished the Supreme Court's authority to review his decrees
- Issued thousands of presidential decrees with the force of law
- Revised the constitution in 1973 to remove term limits and consolidate executive power

CRONYISM AND ECONOMIC MISMANAGEMENT

Marcos used his power to transfer control of major Philippine industries to his family and close associates. His cronies received monopolies over sugar trading, coconut levies, banking, telecommunications, and other sectors. Government contracts and loans flowed to crony businesses regardless of performance or qualifications.

Imelda Marcos, his wife, became a force in her own right — serving as Governor of Metro Manila and Minister of Human Settlements, while earning a reputation for extravagant spending. Her collection of over 3,000 pairs of shoes, discovered when the Marcos family fled in 1986, became an international symbol of the regime's excess against a backdrop of widespread poverty.

The Philippines' foreign debt grew from under $1 billion in 1965 to $26 billion by 1986. A debt crisis in the early 1980s, combined with the political instability following the Aquino assassination, sent the economy into severe contraction. GDP fell by 7 percent in 1984 and a further 4 percent in 1985.

THE OPPOSITION AND HUMAN RIGHTS ABUSES

Documented human rights abuses under the Marcos regime include:

- 3,257 extrajudicial killings
- 35,000 cases of torture
- 737 enforced disappearances
- Over 70,000 incarcerations without trial

Prominent opponents who were imprisoned included Benigno Aquino Jr., Jose Diokno, and Sergio Osmena Jr. The Catholic Church, under Cardinal Jaime Sin, became an increasingly important voice of opposition.

THE ASSASSINATION OF NINOY AQUINO (1983)

The event that broke the Marcos regime's grip on public opinion was the assassination of Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr. on August 21, 1983. Aquino had been held in prison for nearly eight years before being allowed to travel to the United States for heart surgery. Warned that his return would be dangerous, he came back anyway.

He was shot dead on the tarmac of Manila International Airport seconds after stepping off the plane, with journalists and cameras present. The gunman was immediately killed. A government inquiry blamed a communist conspiracy. Most Filipinos and international observers did not believe it.

The funeral drew an estimated two million people through the streets of Manila. The assassination made Aquino a martyr and transformed his widow, Corazon, from a private citizen into the symbol of the opposition.

THE END OF THE MARCOS ERA

The snap election of February 7, 1986, the defection of Enrile and Ramos, and the People Power Revolution of February 22-25, 1986 ended Marcos's rule. He and his family fled to Hawaii on February 25, 1986, aboard US military aircraft. Marcos died in Honolulu on September 28, 1989.

He left behind a country with a devastated economy, hollowed democratic institutions, and a deeply damaged civil society. The Marcos family returned to Philippine politics after his death. His son, Ferdinand Marcos Jr., was elected president of the Philippines in 2022.

In 2021, the Philippine government issued a hero's burial for Marcos at the Libingan ng mga Bayani — a decision that drew protests from human rights groups and survivors of Martial Law.
