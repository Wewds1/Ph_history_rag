THE PHILIPPINE ECONOMY: A HISTORICAL OVERVIEW
==============================================

THE COLONIAL ECONOMY (1565-1946)

The Spanish colonial economy was built around extraction and trade rather than development. The Manila Galleon Trade (1565-1815) was the economic backbone of colonial Manila — Chinese goods were sold in Acapulco for silver, which flowed back to China. The Philippines itself was largely a way station. Agricultural production was secondary, driven by forced labor under the encomienda and polo systems.

In the 19th century, the Philippine economy began opening to world trade. Governor-General Basco y Vargas established the Economic Society of Friends of the Country in 1781 and the tobacco monopoly, forcing farmers in Cagayan and Ilocos to grow tobacco for the colonial government. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 shortened the voyage from the Philippines to Europe and accelerated the integration of the Philippine economy into global commodity markets.

Sugar, tobacco, abaca (Manila hemp), and coconut became the major cash crops. Large landholdings — haciendas — controlled by the Spanish friar orders and wealthy ilustrado families came to dominate agricultural production. This pattern of concentrated land ownership in the hands of a small elite persisted long after independence.

THE AMERICAN PERIOD AND TRADE DEPENDENCY

American colonial policy tied the Philippine economy closely to American markets. The 1909 Payne-Aldrich Act allowed most Philippine goods to enter the United States duty-free. This benefited Filipino landowners who exported sugar and coconut products but discouraged industrialization — why build factories when preferential access to American markets rewarded raw material exports?

By independence in 1946, the Philippines had a weak industrial base, a land tenure system weighted toward large landowners, and an economy heavily dependent on the United States as both export market and source of investment. The Bell Trade Act of 1946 extended free trade between the two countries for eight years and gave American citizens equal rights with Filipinos in exploiting Philippine natural resources — a condition attached to the $620 million war rehabilitation payment.

EARLY INDEPENDENCE: IMPORT SUBSTITUTION (1946-1960s)

The Quirino and Magsaysay administrations faced a foreign exchange crisis in the early 1950s. The government responded with import controls and a policy of import-substitution industrialization — protecting domestic manufacturers from foreign competition by restricting imports. Local industries grew, but they were often inefficient, relying on protected markets rather than competitive quality.

Carlos Garcia's Filipino First Policy (1957-1961) extended this protectionist approach, restricting foreign ownership and reserving key economic activities for Filipino citizens. Diosdado Macapagal liberalized the economy in 1962, removing import controls and devaluing the peso.

THE MARCOS ERA: DEBT-FINANCED GROWTH AND COLLAPSE

Marcos came to power promising modernization. His early years saw genuine infrastructure investment and green revolution agricultural programs that increased rice production. Philippine GDP grew at reasonable rates through the late 1960s.

Martial Law initially attracted foreign investment and enabled Marcos to implement economic decisions quickly, without legislative constraint. But the concentration of economic power in the hands of cronies — monopolies in sugar trading, coconut levies, banking, and telecommunications — hollowed out the economy over time.

When global oil prices spiked in 1973 and 1979, the Philippines, like many developing countries, borrowed heavily from international banks flush with petrodollars. The foreign debt grew from under $1 billion in 1965 to $26 billion by 1986. Debt servicing consumed 40 percent of government expenditure by the mid-1980s.

The Aquino assassination in 1983 triggered a capital flight crisis. The peso collapsed. The economy shrank by 7 percent in 1984 and 4 percent in 1985. The Philippines became one of the few countries in Asia to experience a significant economic decline during the 1970s and 1980s — a period when its neighbors like South Korea, Taiwan, and Thailand were growing rapidly.

POST-MARCOS RECOVERY AND THE OFW ECONOMY

Corazon Aquino inherited an economy in crisis and a debt burden that absorbed most of government revenues. Despite promising conditions, her administration did not achieve the structural reforms — particularly land reform — needed to address the inequality underlying the economy's problems.

The 1990s brought modest liberalization. Fidel Ramos privatized state corporations, opened the telecommunications market, and presided over a period of GDP growth in the range of 5-6 percent annually. But the Asian Financial Crisis of 1997-1998 hit the Philippines hard, though somewhat less severely than Thailand, Indonesia, and South Korea.

The Overseas Filipino Worker (OFW) economy became an increasingly important stabilizer. Millions of Filipinos took jobs abroad — in the Middle East, Hong Kong, Singapore, the United States, Europe — and sent money home. Remittances grew from around $1 billion in the mid-1980s to over $36 billion annually by the 2020s, consistently representing 8-10 percent of GDP. The Philippines became one of the world's largest recipients of overseas remittances. This provided households with income and the country with foreign exchange, but it also reflected a failure to generate enough good jobs at home.

21ST CENTURY: BPO AND GROWTH

The business process outsourcing (BPO) industry became one of the Philippine economy's major growth drivers in the 2000s and 2010s. English proficiency, a large educated workforce, a compatible time zone for American business hours, and relatively low labor costs made the Philippines a leading destination for call centers and back-office operations. By the 2010s, the Philippines had surpassed India as the world's largest call center industry by headcount.

The Aquino III administration (2010-2016) saw some of the fastest economic growth in the country's history — GDP growth averaged about 6.3 percent annually — driven by the BPO sector, strong remittance inflows, and improving public finances.

PERSISTENT CHALLENGES

Despite periods of growth, several structural problems have persisted across administrations:

- Land inequality: the land reform programs launched under Aquino and continued through subsequent administrations have been partial and incomplete. The landed elite has consistently resisted genuine redistribution.
- Infrastructure deficit: decades of underinvestment left the Philippines with roads, ports, and public transit systems inadequate for its growing urban population. Manila's traffic congestion is among the worst in the world.
- Corruption: the Philippines consistently scores poorly on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index, ranking among the lower half of Asian countries.
- Regional inequality: economic activity remains heavily concentrated in Metro Manila and a handful of other urban centers, while rural and remote regions lag significantly.
- The middle-income trap: the Philippines has struggled to move up the manufacturing value chain, stuck between low-wage competitors and more advanced industrial economies.
