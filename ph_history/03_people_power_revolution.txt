THE PEOPLE POWER REVOLUTION (EDSA 1986)
========================================

BACKGROUND: THE MARCOS DICTATORSHIP

Ferdinand Marcos was first elected president of the Philippines in 1965. He won re-election in 1969, becoming the first Philippine president to serve a second term.

On September 21, 1972, Marcos declared Martial Law under Proclamation 1081, citing the threats of communist insurgency and Muslim separatism in the south. In practice, Martial Law allowed him to rule by decree, suspend the constitution, abolish Congress, detain political opponents without trial, and control the press. The 1973 Constitution he promulgated removed presidential term limits, extending his hold on power indefinitely.

His regime was marked by:
- An estimated 3,257 documented extrajudicial killings
- 35,000 documented cases of torture
- 737 people who "disappeared"
- More than 70,000 incarcerations
- Massive corruption and cronyism, with public contracts funneled to Marcos allies
- A foreign debt that climbed from $360 million in 1962 to $26 billion by 1986

THE ASSASSINATION OF NINOY AQUINO (1983)

Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr. was Marcos's most prominent political opponent, a senator who had been jailed for years. After years in prison, he was allowed to travel to the United States for heart surgery. On August 21, 1983, he returned to the Philippines despite warnings that his life was in danger.

He was shot dead on the tarmac of Manila International Airport minutes after landing, before a crowd of journalists and onlookers. The gunman was immediately killed by military escorts, preventing any interrogation. The assassination was widely attributed to Marcos or his allies, though a government investigation initially blamed a communist conspiracy.

Aquino's murder galvanized the opposition and shocked the international community. His funeral drew more than two million mourners through the streets of Manila. Yellow, the color of the ribbon that Ninoy's supporters wore, became the symbol of opposition to Marcos.

THE SNAP ELECTION (1986)

By 1985, Marcos was facing mounting pressure from the United States to demonstrate democratic legitimacy. In a surprise move, he announced a "snap election" to be held in February 1986. The opposition coalesced behind Corazon "Cory" Aquino, Ninoy's widow — a housewife with no prior political experience who had been drafted into candidacy by a petition of over a million signatures.

The election on February 7, 1986 was marred by violence and fraud. Government computer technicians walked out in protest after being ordered to manipulate vote tallies. The National Assembly, controlled by Marcos, declared him the winner. Both candidates held their own inauguration rallies.

THE FOUR DAYS OF EDSA (FEBRUARY 22-25, 1986)

February 22: Defense Minister Juan Ponce Enrile — a longtime Marcos ally who had helped implement Martial Law — and Armed Forces Vice Chief of Staff Fidel Ramos publicly announced their defection from the Marcos regime. They barricaded themselves at Camp Aguinaldo in Quezon City. Cardinal Jaime Sin, the Archbishop of Manila, broadcast a message on Radio Veritas urging Filipinos to go to EDSA to protect the defectors. Hundreds of thousands of people responded.

February 23: The crowds on Epifanio de los Santos Avenue (EDSA) swelled to over a million. Civilian protesters — nuns, students, workers, priests, families — blocked the path of tanks sent to crush the revolt. Some knelt in prayer in front of armored vehicles. Others offered food and flowers to soldiers. Most of Marcos's troops refused to fire on the crowd.

February 24: More military units defected. Aquino held a press conference in Cebu calling on all Filipinos to support the uprising. By midday, 300,000 to 500,000 people were on EDSA, with millions more across the country.

February 25: Both Aquino and Marcos were inaugurated as president — Aquino at Club Filipino in San Juan, Marcos at Malacañang Palace. No foreign ambassadors attended Marcos's ceremony. The United States, which had long supported Marcos, sent word through Senator Paul Laxalt: "Cut and cut cleanly." Marcos and his family, along with close associates, fled to Clark Air Base and then to Hawaii aboard US military aircraft. He died in exile in Honolulu on September 28, 1989.

SIGNIFICANCE

The EDSA Revolution was notable for what it was not: it was not a military coup, and it was not a violent uprising. It was a mass civilian action that succeeded because enough soldiers refused to shoot their own people.

It had immediate effects beyond the Philippines. The peaceful overthrow of an entrenched authoritarian ruler by unarmed civilians inspired pro-democracy movements in South Korea, Taiwan, Indonesia, and later Eastern Europe. The broadcast of the Philippine People Power Revolution was inscribed in the UNESCO Memory of the World Register in 2003.

Corazon Aquino served as president until 1992. She restored democratic institutions, released political prisoners, held free elections, and allowed a new constitution to be drafted and ratified. Her presidency was not without difficulty — she survived seven coup attempts — but the democratic transition held.

The revolution's limitations were also real. The oligarchic economic structures that had allowed Marcos to consolidate power were not fundamentally dismantled. Land reform, one of Aquino's central promises, stalled on her own family's vast sugar estate, Hacienda Luisita. Many of the political dynasties that had aligned with Marcos simply realigned with Aquino and continued to dominate Philippine politics.

The most striking recent postscript: in 2022, Ferdinand Marcos Jr. — son of the deposed dictator — was elected president of the Philippines by a wide margin.
