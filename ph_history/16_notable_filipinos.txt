NOTABLE FILIPINOS IN HISTORY
=============================

ANDRES BONIFACIO (1863-1897)

Andres Bonifacio was born in Tondo, Manila, to a working-class family. He was largely self-educated, reading the works of Jose Rizal, Victor Hugo, and accounts of the French Revolution. He worked as a warehouse clerk and street vendor.

In 1892, after Rizal's arrest and exile, Bonifacio founded the Katipunan — the secret revolutionary society dedicated to independence from Spain through armed revolt. He built its membership to an estimated 100,000 before the revolution was exposed in 1896. He led the first armed engagements of the Philippine Revolution.

Bonifacio represents the popular, working-class current in Philippine nationalism — distinct from the educated ilustrado reformers like Rizal. His approach was direct: Spain would not reform, so it had to be expelled.

At the Tejeros Convention in 1897, the revolutionary leaders chose Emilio Aguinaldo as their president. Bonifacio contested the result and attempted to form a rival government. He was arrested on Aguinaldo's orders, tried for treason, and executed on May 10, 1897, in the mountains of Cavite. He was 33 years old.

His legacy is contested: celebrated by labor and nationalist movements as the true voice of the Filipino masses, overshadowed in official Philippine history by Rizal (his execution having been ordered by the government that named him national hero). November 30, the day of his birth, is celebrated as Bonifacio Day.

APOLINARIO MABINI (1864-1903)

Apolinario Mabini was the ideological brain of the Philippine Revolution and the First Philippine Republic. Born to a poor family in Batangas, he studied law and was the finest constitutional thinker in the revolutionary movement. He drafted the Malolos Constitution and served as the first Prime Minister of the First Philippine Republic under Aguinaldo.

Mabini contracted polio in 1896 and was partially paralyzed from the waist down. He continued to serve the Republic despite his disability, earning the nickname "The Sublime Paralytic." He refused to swear allegiance to the United States after the Philippine-American War and was exiled to Guam by the Americans in 1901. He returned to the Philippines in 1903 and died of cholera shortly after.

His writings, including La Revolucion Filipina, remain important documents of Philippine political thought.

GABRIELA SILANG (1731-1763)

Maria Josefa Gabriela Cariño Silang was one of the most significant figures of the 18th-century resistance to Spanish rule in the Ilocos region. Her husband, Diego Silang, led a revolt against Spanish authority in Ilocos in 1762, taking advantage of the British occupation of Manila. After Diego was assassinated in 1763, Gabriela took command of the rebel forces — the only woman to lead a major armed resistance against Spanish colonialism in the Philippines.

She led continued resistance for two months before being captured. She was hanged in Vigan on September 20, 1763. She is remembered as a feminist icon and national heroine. The Gabriela women's alliance — a major progressive women's organization in the Philippines — takes its name from her.

MELCHORA AQUINO (TANDANG SORA) (1812-1919)

Melchora Aquino, known as Tandang Sora ("Old Sora"), was a market vendor from Balintawak, Caloocan, who provided food, shelter, and medical care to the Katipunan fighters during the early days of the 1896 revolution. She was over 80 years old when she participated in the revolution.

The Spanish captured her, tortured her trying to extract information about the Katipunan, and when she refused to cooperate, exiled her to the Marianas Islands. She returned to the Philippines at 95 years old after the revolution succeeded and lived to be 107. She is revered as the "Mother of the Katipunan."

ANTONIO LUNA (1866-1899)

Antonio Luna was the most brilliant military commander of the Philippine-American War. Trained as a pharmacist in Spain, he became a general who implemented modern military strategy and discipline against American forces. He reorganized the Philippine Army, enforced strict military codes, and adopted guerrilla tactics combined with conventional defense that gave the Americans their most difficult early battles.

Luna was killed on June 5, 1899, at the convent of Cabanatuan. The circumstances remain disputed — he was shot by soldiers loyal to Aguinaldo after a confrontation. Many historians believe Aguinaldo ordered or allowed his assassination. Luna's death deprived the Philippine Army of its most capable commander at a critical point in the war.

SULTAN KUDARAT (c. 1581-1671)

Muhammad Dipatuan Kudarat was the Sultan of Maguindanao who led the most sustained Muslim resistance to Spanish colonization in Mindanao during the 17th century. He united various Mindanao sultans and datus against repeated Spanish military campaigns, building a coalition that repelled Spanish forces from the Mindanao interior for decades.

He is recognized as a national hero and his name is given to a province in Mindanao. His significance is partly symbolic — as a Filipino hero who was neither Christian nor from Luzon, he represents the breadth of the Philippine national story.

DATU LAPULAPU (c. 1491-unknown)

Lapulapu was the ruler of Mactan island, Cebu, who killed Ferdinand Magellan on April 27, 1521 — making him the first Filipino to successfully resist foreign conquest. He refused the authority of both Magellan and his ally, Rajah Humabon of Cebu, and led a force of warriors against a Spanish landing party.

Almost nothing is known about Lapulapu's life before or after Mactan. The few descriptions come from Antonio Pigafetta, the Italian chronicler of Magellan's expedition. Despite this, Lapulapu has become the preeminent symbol of Filipino resistance to colonialism. His statue stands in Mactan near the site of the battle. The lapu-lapu, a popular Philippine food fish (grouper), is named after him.

CORAZON AQUINO (1933-2009)

Corazon "Cory" Aquino was the 11th President of the Philippines and the first woman to hold the office. Born to one of the Philippines' most prominent political families, she lived as a housewife until the assassination of her husband, Ninoy Aquino, in 1983 transformed her into a political figure.

Her unlikely candidacy in the 1986 snap election galvanized the opposition to Marcos. Her inauguration after the People Power Revolution on February 25, 1986 was one of the defining moments of 20th-century Asian history. Her presidency restored democratic institutions in the Philippines after 14 years of authoritarian rule.

She survived seven coup attempts and faced persistent criticism that her economic policies favored the elite. But the democratic transition she oversaw held. She did not seek re-election in 1992, honoring the one-term limit in the new constitution.

She was diagnosed with colon cancer in 2008 and died on August 1, 2009. Her funeral drew enormous crowds. She remains one of the most beloved figures in modern Philippine history.
